1. Introduction & Historical Evolution

Module 1 of 8

The United Nations (UN) is the world's foremost intergovernmental organization, tasked with maintaining international peace and security, fostering friendly relations among nations, achieving international cooperation, and harmonizing the actions of nations. Established in the catastrophic aftermath of World War II, it was conceived to succeed where its predecessor, the League of Nations, had fundamentally collapsed.

The Demise of the League of Nations

Formed under the Treaty of Versailles (1919) following Woodrow Wilson's Fourteen Points, the League of Nations was crippled from its inception by fatal institutional and geopolitical flaws:

  • Absence of the United States: Despite President Wilson championing the League, the US Senate refused ratification, depriving the League of the premier rising economic and naval superpower.
  • The Unanimity Rule: The League Covenant required unanimous decisions in both the Assembly and the Council, granting any single state an effective veto to block collective action.
  • Inability to Halt Fascist Aggression: The League failed catastrophically during the Japanese invasion of Manchuria (1931), the Italian invasion of Abyssinia/Ethiopia (1935), and the German reoccupation of the Rhineland (1936), rendering it completely obsolete by the outbreak of World War II in September 1939.

Stepping Stones to the San Francisco Charter (1941–1945)

The United Nations was constructed through seven decisive wartime diplomatic conferences between the Allied powers:

Date Conference / Accord Key Leaders / Signatories Significance & Exam Takeaway
June 12, 1941 Declaration of St. James's Palace UK, Canada, Australia, NZ, South Africa, European exiled governments First joint pledge for enduring peace founded on free peoples working together without threat of aggression.
August 14, 1941 The Atlantic Charter Franklin D. Roosevelt (USA) & Winston Churchill (UK) Drafted aboard warships off Newfoundland; laid down 8 principles: self-determination, freedom of seas, disarmament, and global economic cooperation.
January 1, 1942 Declaration by United Nations 26 Allied Nations (Washington, D.C.) President Roosevelt coined the name "United Nations"; pledged full employment of resources against the Axis and no separate peace.
Oct–Dec 1943 Moscow & Tehran Conferences The "Big Three" (Roosevelt, Churchill, Stalin) + China Formally recognized the necessity of establishing at the earliest practicable date a general international organization based on sovereign equality.
Aug–Oct 1944 Dumbarton Oaks Conference USA, UK, USSR, Republic of China (Washington, D.C.) Blueprinted the UN architecture: Formulated the General Assembly, Security Council, Secretariat, and International Court of Justice.
February 1945 Yalta Conference (Crimea) Roosevelt, Churchill, Stalin Formulated the Security Council voting formula (The Veto Power) and resolved that trusteeship would replace the League mandate system.
Apr 25 – Jun 26, 1945 UNCIO (San Francisco Conference) Delegates of 50 nations (850 delegates) Charter signed unanimously on June 26, 1945. Poland signed later on Oct 15, 1945, becoming the 51st original member state.
October 24, 1945 UN Charter Enters Into Force Big Five + Majority of Signatories Ratification completed; October 24 is commemorated globally as United Nations Day.
September 30, 1947 Pakistan's UN Admission UNGA Resolution 29 Pakistan was admitted as the 57th member of the UN; Sir Muhammad Zafarullah Khan represented Pakistan.
High-Yield Exam Note: Original Members Count
While 50 nations signed the Charter at the conclusion of the San Francisco Conference on June 26, 1945, Poland was unable to send a delegation due to dispute over its government composition. A space was reserved for Poland, which signed on October 15, 1945. Therefore, the official count of founding original member states of the UN is 51.

2. Headquarters, Structure & Charter Anatomy

Module 2 of 8

Headquarters & Extraterritorial Legal Status

The permanent headquarters of the United Nations is located on an 17-acre tract in Turtle Bay, Manhattan, New York City, USA, bounded by First Avenue, 42nd Street, 48th Street, and the East River. The land was purchased with an $8.5 million donation from John D. Rockefeller Jr. and designed by an international board of architects including Oscar Niemeyer and Le Corbusier.

  • Extraterritoriality: Under the 1947 Headquarters Agreement between the US and the UN, the complex is an extraterritorial international zone. It has its own dedicated security force, fire service, and issues its own postage stamps (denominated in US Dollars, Swiss Francs, and Euros) valid only on mail posted from UN headquarters buildings.
  • Major Duty Stations (UN Offices Away from HQ):
    • UNOG (Geneva, Switzerland): Housed in the historic Palais des Nations; global hub for human rights and disarmament diplomacy.
    • UNOV (Vienna, Austria): Located at Vienna International Centre; hosts IAEA, UNODC, and outer space affairs.
    • UNON (Nairobi, Kenya): Located in Gigiri, Nairobi; the only UN headquarters situated in Africa and the Global South, hosting UNEP and UN-Habitat.

Official and Working Languages

The United Nations recognizes six official languages:

1. Arabic (added 1973 for GA, 1982 for SC)
2. Chinese
3. English
4. French
5. Russian
6. Spanish

Crucial Exam Distinction: While there are 6 official languages in which all formal documents and speeches are translated, the Secretariat uses only two working languages: English and French.

Anatomy of the UN Charter (19 Chapters & 111 Articles)

The Charter is the constituent treaty of the organization. By virtue of Article 103 (The Supremacy Clause), obligations under the UN Charter prevail over any obligations incurred under any other international treaty.

Chapter / Article Subject Matter Constitutional & Legal Significance for CSS / PMS
Article 1 Purposes of the United Nations Maintains peace, develops friendly relations on equal rights and self-determination, achieves international cooperation in socio-economic-cultural spheres.
Article 2 Principles of the Organization 2(1): Sovereign equality of all members.
2(3): Settlement of disputes by peaceful means.
2(4): Prohibition of the threat or use of force against territorial integrity or political independence.
2(7): The Domestic Jurisdiction clause: nothing authorizes the UN to intervene in matters essentially within the domestic jurisdiction of any state.
Chapter VI (Arts 33–38) Pacific Settlement of Disputes Envisages peaceful dispute resolution via negotiation, inquiry, mediation, conciliation, arbitration, or judicial settlement. Resolutions passed under Chapter VI are recommendations and are non-binding. (Crucial context for Kashmir resolutions 47 and 91).
Chapter VII (Arts 39–50) Action with Respect to Threats to Peace & Aggression Legally binding enforcement architecture. Art 39 determines existence of threat; Art 41 authorizes non-military sanctions (economic embargoes, severance of diplomatic ties); Art 42 authorizes military action by air, sea, or land forces.
Article 51 Inherent Right of Self-Defence Explicitly preserves the inherent right of individual or collective self-defence if an armed attack occurs against a member state, until the Security Council has taken measures necessary to maintain international peace.
Article 99 Secretary-General's Prerogative Grants the Secretary-General explicit political power to bring to the attention of the Security Council any matter which in his opinion may threaten international peace. (Invoked in Congo 1960, Lebanon 1989, and Gaza 2023).
Article 103 Supremacy Clause In the event of a conflict between the obligations of the Members under the UN Charter and their obligations under any other international agreement, their obligations under the Charter shall prevail.
Chapter XVIII (Arts 108–109) Charter Amendments Amendments require adoption by a two-thirds vote of the General Assembly and ratification by two-thirds of the member states, including all five permanent members of the Security Council (giving each P5 an absolute veto over reform).

3. Principal Organs & Specialized Agencies

Module 3 of 8

Article 7 of the UN Charter establishes six principal organs. In addition, the broader UN system comprises 15 autonomous specialized agencies, multiple specialized programmes and funds, and related treaty bodies.

General Assembly (UNGA)

Deliberative & Policy Organ

The only universal organ where all 193 member states enjoy equal representation (one state, one vote). Convenes annually in September for the General Debate.

  • Voting: Simple majority for ordinary matters; 2/3 majority for "Important Questions" (budget, peace, admitting members).
  • Uniting for Peace (Res 377A, 1950): Empowers GA to act if UNSC fails to exercise responsibility due to veto.
  • Current President: Philemon Yang (Cameroon, 79th Session 2024–2025).

Security Council (UNSC)

Primary Peace & Security Organ

Has primary responsibility for the maintenance of international peace and security under Article 24. It is the only organ whose resolutions members are legally obligated to obey (Art 25).

  • Composition: 15 members — 5 Permanent (China, France, Russia, UK, USA) + 10 Non-Permanent elected for 2-year terms.
  • Voting Rule: 9 affirmative votes. On substantive matters, requires concurring votes of all P5 (The Veto Power).
  • Pakistan: Elected for 2025–2026 (8th historical term).

Economic & Social Council (ECOSOC)

Development & Human Rights Hub

Coordinates economic, social, environmental, and cultural activities. Guides 5 Regional Commissions (ESCAP, ESCWA, ECLAC, UNECE, ECA) and over 5,000 NGOs with consultative status.

  • Composition: 54 member states elected by UNGA for 3-year staggered terms (18 elected annually).
  • Voting: Simple majority rule.
  • Pakistan's Leadership: Ambassador Munir Akram served twice as ECOSOC President (2005 and 2020–2021).

International Court of Justice (ICJ)

Principal Judicial Organ ("World Court")

Settles legal disputes between sovereign states and gives advisory opinions on legal questions submitted by authorized UN organs and specialized agencies.

  • Seat: Peace Palace, The Hague, Netherlands (only principal organ not in New York).
  • Composition: 15 judges elected for 9-year terms by concurrent absolute majorities in UNGA and UNSC.
  • Pakistani Legend: Sir Zafarullah Khan served as Judge (1954–61) and President of ICJ (1970–73).

The Secretariat

Executive Civil Service

The administrative backbone of the UN, employing tens of thousands of international civil servants. Administers peacekeeping operations, translates documents, and surveys economic trends.

  • Leadership: Headed by the Secretary-General, appointed by UNGA upon recommendation of UNSC for a 5-year renewable term.
  • Current SG: António Guterres (Portugal, assumed office Jan 1, 2017; re-elected 2021).

Trusteeship Council

Decolonization Organ (Suspended)

Established under Chapter XIII to supervise the administration of 11 Trust Territories and guide them toward self-government or independence.

  • Historical Mission Accomplished: All 11 trust territories attained independence.
  • Suspended Operations: November 1, 1994, after the independence of Palau (the last trust territory). Now meets only on request.

The 15 UN Specialized Agencies (Master Table)

Specialized agencies are autonomous intergovernmental organizations working with the UN through negotiated relationship agreements coordinated by ECOSOC.

Acronym Organization Name Headquarters Year Founded Mandate & High-Yield Exam Focus
WHO World Health Organization Geneva, Switzerland 1948 Directing authority on international health; coordinates global responses to pandemics (COVID-19), eradicated smallpox (1980), World Health Day April 7.
ILO International Labour Organization Geneva, Switzerland 1919 (Versailles) Tripartite structure (governments, employers, workers); sets international labour standards; won Nobel Peace Prize in 1969 on 50th anniversary.
UNESCO UN Educational, Scientific & Cultural Org Paris, France 1945 Promotes world peace through international cooperation in education, science, culture; designates World Heritage Sites (e.g., Mohenjo-daro, Taxila).
FAO Food and Agriculture Organization Rome, Italy 1945 Leads international efforts to defeat hunger and achieve food security; World Food Day observed annually on October 16.
ICAO International Civil Aviation Organization Montreal, Canada 1944 (Chicago Conv) Codifies principles and techniques of international air navigation and flight safety across 193 member states.
IMO International Maritime Organization London, United Kingdom 1948 (in force 1958) Regulates shipping safety, maritime security, and prevention of marine pollution by ships (SOLAS & MARPOL conventions).
ITU International Telecommunication Union Geneva, Switzerland 1865 Oldest specialized agency in UN system; allocates global radio spectrum, satellite orbits, and technical telecommunication standards.
UPU Universal Postal Union Bern, Switzerland 1874 (Treaty of Bern) Coordinates postal policies among member nations; World Post Day October 9.
WMO World Meteorological Organization Geneva, Switzerland 1950 Authoritative voice on Earth's atmosphere, weather, climate change, and water resources; co-founded IPCC with UNEP.
WIPO World Intellectual Property Organization Geneva, Switzerland 1967 Administers international treaties protecting intellectual property (patents, copyrights, trademarks, industrial designs).

Essential Distinction: Related Bodies vs Specialized Agencies
The International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) based in Vienna and the Organization for the Prohibition of Chemical Weapons (OPCW) in The Hague are autonomous intergovernmental organizations that report directly to UNGA and UNSC, but they are technically related organizations, not specialized agencies under Articles 57 and 63. Similarly, the World Trade Organization (WTO) in Geneva is an independent treaty-based trade organization outside the formal UN specialized agency umbrella.

4. Important Facts, Chronology & Pakistan at the UN

Complete Chronological Roll of UN Secretaries-General

The Secretary-General is the "chief administrative officer" of the Organization (Article 97) and the world's top diplomat.

No. Secretary-General Country Term in Office Key Global Milestones & Tragedies
1 Trygve Lie Norway 1946–1952 First Secretary-General; supported UN intervention in the Korean War (1950); resigned under Soviet boycott.
2 Dag Hammarskjöld Sweden 1953–1961 Pioneered preventative diplomacy and UN peacekeeping during Suez Crisis (1956); died in a tragic plane crash in Ndola, Northern Rhodesia (Zambia) on Sept 18, 1961; awarded the posthumous Nobel Peace Prize in 1961.
3 U Thant Burma (Myanmar) 1961–1971 First Asian / non-European Secretary-General; mediated during Cuban Missile Crisis (1962) and 1965 India-Pakistan war.
4 Kurt Waldheim Austria 1972–1981 Two full terms; oversaw Cyprus crisis and Yom Kippur war peacekeeping; later elected President of Austria.
5 Javier Pérez de Cuéllar Peru 1982–1991 First South American SG; mediated end of Iran-Iraq War (1988) and negotiated peace accords in Central America; oversaw end of Cold War.
6 Boutros Boutros-Ghali Egypt 1992–1996 Authored seminal report An Agenda for Peace; tenure overshadowed by Rwandan Genocide (1994) and Srebrenica massacre (1995); served single term due to US veto.
7 Kofi Annan Ghana 1997–2006 First Secretary-General from sub-Saharan Africa rising through Secretariat ranks; launched Millennium Development Goals (MDGs) and Global Compact; awarded Nobel Peace Prize jointly with the UN in 2001.
8 Ban Ki-moon South Korea 2007–2016 Championed the 2030 Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), Paris Climate Accord (2015), and established UN Women.
9 António Guterres Portugal 2017–Present Former UN High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR) and Prime Minister of Portugal; spearheaded Summit of the Future (2024); invoked Article 99 on Gaza in Dec 2023.

Pakistan and the United Nations: Historical Record

Pakistan joined the United Nations on September 30, 1947, shortly after gaining independence on August 14, 1947. Over nearly eight decades, Pakistan has maintained an exceptional diplomatic and operational record:

  • 8 Terms on the UN Security Council: Pakistan has been elected as a non-permanent member of the UNSC eight times: 1952–53, 1968–69, 1976–77, 1983–84, 1993–94, 2003–04, 2012–13, and most recently elected on June 6, 2024 for the 2025–2026 term with an overwhelming 182 votes out of 193 in the General Assembly.
  • Diplomatic Stalwarts in Principal Organs:
    • Sir Muhammad Zafarullah Khan: Pakistan's first Foreign Minister; elected President of the 17th Session of the UN General Assembly (1962–63); served as Judge of the International Court of Justice (1954–61), Vice President (1958–61), and President of the ICJ (1970–73).
    • Prof. Ahmad Shah Patras Bokhari: Served as Pakistan's Permanent Representative (1951–54) where his oratory on Tunisian and Moroccan independence won global acclaim; served as UN Under-Secretary-General for Public Information (1953–1958).
    • Ambassador Munir Akram: Longest-serving Permanent Representative; twice elected President of ECOSOC (in 2005 and 2020–2021).
  • UN Peacekeeping Flag-Bearer: Pakistan is historically one of the world's top troop- and police-contributing countries (TCCs). Over 200,000 Pakistani peacekeepers have served across 46 missions in 29 countries. More than 170 Pakistani peacekeepers have sacrificed their lives under the blue helmet (including the heroic rescue of trapped US Rangers in Mogadishu, Somalia in October 1993, and missions in Congo, Bosnia, Liberia, and CAR).
  • The Jammu & Kashmir Dispute: First brought to the UNSC in January 1948. The Council passed Resolution 47 (April 21, 1948) establishing the UN Commission for India and Pakistan (UNCIP) and directing a free and impartial plebiscite. UNMOGIP (UN Military Observer Group in India and Pakistan) remains deployed along the Line of Control to supervise the ceasefire.

5. Related Current Affairs (2024–2026)

Module 5 of 8

For CSS Current Affairs and International Relations, understanding modern structural paralysis and diplomatic flashpoints is mandatory.

1. The Summit of the Future & "Pact for the Future" (September 2024)

Convened at UN Headquarters in New York during the 79th UNGA, world leaders adopted the milestone Pact for the Future along with two annexes: the Global Digital Compact and the Declaration on Future Generations:

  • 56 Action Commitments: Pledges to revitalize multilateralism, reform the outdated international financial architecture (Bretton Woods institutions), accelerate the 2030 SDGs, and overhaul the UN Security Council.
  • Global Digital Compact: The first comprehensive global framework for digital technologies, artificial intelligence governance, and digital public goods, ensuring that AI development is governed by human rights and international law.
  • Debt Relief for Developing States: Strong call for restructuring the sovereign debt architecture and expanding access to Special Drawing Rights (SDRs) for climate-vulnerable economies.

The structural paralysis of the Security Council during the Ukraine war and Gaza conflict has intensified the reform debate. The main competing coalitions are:

Bloc / Group Core Member States Proposed Reform Formula & Veto Stand
The G4 Coalition India, Brazil, Germany, Japan Demands expansion of both permanent and non-permanent seats; seek permanent seats with veto power for themselves.
Uniting for Consensus (UfC) / "Coffee Club" Led by Italy, Pakistan, Mexico, Argentina, South Korea, Canada, Turkey, Spain Strictly opposes creating new permanent seats or expanding the undemocratic veto. Proposes adding 10–11 non-permanent, elected, and accountable seats with longer or renewable terms to democratize representation.
Ezulwini Consensus / African Union 54 African Union States Demands at least 2 permanent seats with full veto rights and 5 non-permanent seats for Africa to redress historical injustice.

3. Middle East / Gaza Crisis: Article 99 & UNSC Resolutions

  • Invocation of Article 99 (Dec 6, 2023): Secretary-General António Guterres invoked Article 99 for the first time in his tenure (and first globally since Javier Pérez de Cuéllar in Lebanon in 1989), warning of an imminent catastrophe and complete collapse of public order in the Gaza Strip.
  • UNSC Resolution 2728 (March 25, 2024): Adopted 14-0 (US abstained); demanded an immediate ceasefire for Ramadan leading to a lasting ceasefire, and immediate release of all hostages.
  • UNSC Resolution 2735 (June 10, 2024): Adopted 14-0 (Russia abstained); endorsed a 3-phase comprehensive ceasefire agreement.
  • ICJ Proceedings (*South Africa v. Israel*): South Africa instituted proceedings under the 1948 Genocide Convention. The Court indicated provisional measures ordering the prevention of genocidal acts and unimpeded aid delivery. On July 19, 2024, the ICJ delivered a historic Advisory Opinion declaring Israel's continued presence in the Occupied Palestinian Territory unlawful under international law.

7. Previous Examination Questions (CSS & PMS)

Below are authentic subjective questions from past CSS Competitive Examinations and PMS papers, accompanied by comprehensive analytical model answer outlines prepared by civil service toppers.

CSS Competitive Examination (Current Affairs / IR) Year: 2023 20 Marks
"The veto power of the permanent members in the United Nations Security Council has repeatedly paralyzed the international collective security architecture during major crises (e.g., Ukraine, Middle East). Critically analyze the prospects and structural impediments to reforming the Security Council, evaluating the competing proposals of the G4 and the Uniting for Consensus (UfC) coalition."
Model 20-Mark Answer Framework & Key Headings:
  1. Introduction: Genesis of Article 27(3) at Yalta; the collective security ideal versus realpolitik paralysis; thesis statement on the structural gridlock.
  2. Structural Pathology of the Veto: Historical abuse (USSR/Russia in Eastern Europe, USA in Middle East); violation of sovereign equality (Art 2(1)); how the veto renders Chapter VII enforcement toothless against great powers and their allies.
  3. Evaluation of the G4 Proposal (India, Brazil, Germany, Japan): Arguments for expanding permanent seats (demographic/economic heft); counter-arguments (creating new oligarchies of power, regional rivalries, unlikelihood of current P5 sharing the veto).
  4. Evaluation of the Uniting for Consensus (UfC / Pakistan's) Proposal: Democratizing the Council by adding 10–11 non-permanent, elected, and regional rotating seats; strict opposition to new vetoes; adherence to democratic accountability.
  5. The African Position (Ezulwini Consensus): Demand for 2 permanent seats with veto to correct historical under-representation.
  6. The Primary Constitutional Hurdle (Article 108): Any Charter amendment requires ratification by all P5 members; why no P5 will voluntarily dilute its privileged veto.
  7. Conclusion & Pragmatic Way Forward: Code of conduct on veto restraint in mass atrocities (French-Mexican initiative), strengthening General Assembly via Uniting for Peace (Res 377A), and incremental expansion of non-permanent seats.
CSS Competitive Examination (Pakistan Affairs / IR) Year: 2022 20 Marks
"Evaluate Pakistan's historical contributions to the United Nations since its admission in September 1947, with special reference to UN Peacekeeping Operations, representation in principal organs, and multilateral diplomacy on Kashmir."
Model 20-Mark Answer Framework & Key Headings:
  1. Introduction: Pakistan's adherence to multilateralism as a cornerstone of foreign policy; adherence to the UN Charter principles since Sept 30, 1947.
  2. Operational Contributions to Peacekeeping: Over 200,000 troops deployed across 46 missions; professionalism and supreme sacrifices (170+ martyred); specific case studies: Congo (1960), Somalia rescue (1993), Bosnia, and West Africa.
  3. Diplomatic Leadership in Principal Organs: 8-time elected member of UNSC (including 2025–26); Sir Zafarullah Khan's presidency of UNGA and ICJ; Patras Bokhari as Under-Secretary-General; leadership of G77 and OIC blocs.
  4. Multilateral Advocacy on the Jammu & Kashmir Dispute: Keeping UNSC Resolutions 47, 51, 80, 91 alive; defense of UNMOGIP's legal mandate under the ceasefire line; pushing for right to self-determination (Art 1(2)).
  5. Championing Global Causes: Decolonization of North Africa (Tunisia, Algeria, Morocco), combating Islamophobia (UNGA resolution declaring March 15 International Day to Combat Islamophobia), and climate justice diplomacy at COP27/28.
  6. Conclusion: Pakistan as an indispensable pillar of international law and multilateral peace architecture.
CSS Competitive Examination (International Law) Year: 2021 20 Marks
"Differentiate between the legal enforceability of United Nations resolutions passed under Chapter VI and Chapter VII of the UN Charter. Discuss how this distinction impacts the resolution of the Jammu & Kashmir dispute and the enforcement of ICJ provisional measures."
Model 20-Mark Answer Framework & Key Headings:
  1. Introduction: Theoretical dichotomy between recommendatory Pacific Settlement (Chapter VI) and mandatory Collective Security Enforcement (Chapter VII).
  2. Constitutional Architecture of Chapter VI (Articles 33–38): Non-binding consensual nature; reliance on party consent; resolutions as normative guidance.
  3. Constitutional Architecture of Chapter VII (Articles 39–50): Binding obligations under Article 25; economic embargoes (Art 41) and armed intervention (Art 42); legal consequences of defiance.
  4. Application to the Jammu & Kashmir Dispute: Analysis of UNSC Resolution 47 (1948); Indian argument of non-enforceability under Chapter VI versus Pakistan's position on pacta sunt servanda and international contractual obligations.
  5. Enforcement Mechanics of ICJ Judgments (Article 94): ICJ decisions bind parties (Art 94(1)); recourse to UNSC under Art 94(2) for non-compliance; how the P5 veto impedes enforcement of ICJ provisional measures (e.g., Nicaragua v. US, South Africa v. Israel).
  6. Conclusion: Legal enforceability in international law remains subordinated to great power geopolitical consensus.
PMS Provincial Management Service (Political Science) Year: 2022 20 Marks
"'The United Nations is not created to take humanity to heaven, but to save it from hell.' In light of Dag Hammarskjöld's famous statement, critically evaluate the success and failure of the UN in maintaining global peace over the past eight decades."
Model 20-Mark Answer Framework & Key Headings:
  1. Introduction & Philosophical Context: Deconstructing Hammarskjöld's realism; the UN as a forum of prevention rather than a utopian world government.
  2. Major Historic Successes: Prevention of World War III and nuclear conflagration during Cold War; facilitation of historic decolonization (over 80 nations freed); eradication of smallpox; humanitarian rescues through UNHCR and WFP.
  3. Catastrophic Institutional Failures: The Rwandan Genocide of 1994 (failure of UNAMIR); Srebrenica massacre of 1995 (collapse of UN Safe Area); the 2003 Iraq invasion bypassing UNSC; ongoing inability to stop devastation in Gaza and Ukraine due to vetoes.
  4. Evaluation of Peacekeeping Evolutions: 1st generation peacekeeping (monitoring ceasefires, UNEF I) to 3rd generation multidimensional peace enforcement (MONUSCO).
  5. Synthesis & Verdict: In spite of glaring deficiencies, the collapse of the UN would regress international society into unchecked anarchy; urgency of reforming multilateral architecture to reflect 21st-century multipolarity.
